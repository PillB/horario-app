## Part 1: LatAm SMB Software Buying Process

### 1.1 Who Makes the Decision?

In Latin American SMBs, the **owner/founder is almost universally the sole decision-maker** for technology purchases. Unlike North American companies where IT departments or procurement managers lead software evaluation, LatAm micro and small businesses (5–50 employees) have flat hierarchies.

A Microsoft/Edelman survey of LatAm SME leaders found that the transformation is driven by *líderes y responsables de la toma de decisiones* (leaders and decision-makers) of micro, small, and medium-sized enterprises across diverse economic sectors. In companies at the smaller end of the spectrum, this is almost always the owner/founder.

Spanish-language research on technology management in Mexican SMEs identifies significant variation in sophistication based on company size and location, revealing six patterns of technological management that express differences between micro, small, and medium firms — confirming that tech decisions in micro-firms are made unilaterally by owners, not committees.

**Key implication for Horario:** Your buyer persona is the **restaurant owner, retail store manager, or clinic administrator** — not an IT manager. Messaging must be in plain language, focused on time savings and revenue impact, not technical specs.

### 1.3 Role of Free Trials vs. Freemium vs. Demos

This is one of the most important strategic decisions for Horario's go-to-market. The global SaaS data is instructive:

Freemium self-serve products typically see 3–5% conversion rates, with exceptional performers reaching 6–8%. Sales-assisted freemiums perform better at 5–7% average, and 10–15% for top performers.

Databox found, in a survey conducted with SaaS professionals working with small and medium-sized businesses (SMBs), that free-to-paid conversions ranged between 3–10% on average — and may be even lower for consumer products.

For LatAm SMBs specifically, **freemium with a clear usage cap is superior to time-limited trials** for three reasons:
1. **Trust gap**: SMBs want to validate value before any payment commitment — freemium lets them do so at their own pace.
2. **Informality**: Owners are busy; a 14-day clock creates pressure they resent.
3. **Referral flywheel**: Free users share with peers (especially on WhatsApp groups), generating organic discovery.

**Horario's 1-free-schedule-per-month model is directionally correct** but potentially too restrictive. Consider **1 free AI schedule + read-only access to analytics** to maximize the "aha moment" before the paywall.

The typical SaaS sales cycle runs 84 days overall, but SMB deals under $5K close in approximately 40 days, while enterprise-level sales can stretch beyond 6 months for $100K+ deals.

**The WhatsApp touchpoint compresses the cycle:** A business owner who discovers Horario in a restaurant owners' WhatsApp group, gets a peer recommendation, and downloads the app on their phone can go from zero to paid within a single week — if friction is eliminated.

## Part 2: Price Sensitivity by Country

### 2.1 The LatAm SaaS Price Sensitivity Landscape

Small and medium-sized businesses have limited budgets and are not willing to spend significant amounts of money on technology integration if there is no short-term return on investment (ROI).

Price sensitivity across LatAm SMBs is **uniformly high but varies by country**, largely determined by:
- Average SMB revenue levels
- Macroeconomic stability / currency volatility
- Existing digital tool spend baseline
- Formality level (formal SMBs have more tolerance for recurring SaaS costs)

### 2.2 Country-by-Country Price Sensitivity Ranking

| Country | Price Sensitivity | Key Driver | Relative WTP for $20–$50/mo SaaS |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Peru** | 🔴 Very High | High informality (70.9%), low card penetration, economic volatility | Low–Medium |
| **Colombia** | 🔴 High | Significant informal sector, peso volatility | Low–Medium |
| **Mexico** | 🟠 Medium–High | Larger formal SMB base, but peso risk | Medium |
| **Chile** | 🟡 Medium | Highest income + most formal SMB sector in LatAm | Medium–High |

**Key data points:**

- Ipsos' Global @dvisor survey found that 83% of connected Peruvians believe prices in the country will increase faster than people's income — and 79% believe inflation will be higher in 2024 than the prior year. This macro-anxiety translates directly into resistance to recurring SaaS costs.

- Microsoft's annual survey of LatAm SMEs found that remote work is adopted by less than 15% of companies across all markets, and hybrid work is more prevalent in Brazil, Colombia, and Mexico than Peru. This indicates Peru's SMBs are less digitally mature and therefore more price-resistant to cloud tools.

- Microsoft/Edelman digital transformation surveys identified that Peruvian SMEs rank among countries where data is increasingly used to stay close to customers — 39% in Peru vs. 48% in Argentina and 35% in Brazil — suggesting a widening gap between early digital adopters and laggards.

### 2.3 Price Anchoring Effects in LatAm Markets

The psychological principle of **price anchoring is particularly powerful** in high-price-sensitivity markets like Peru and Colombia. When buyers have weak internal price references for software categories (which most LatAm SMB owners do), they rely heavily on the first price they see.

The higher-priced plan sets an "anchor," making the lower-priced option feel like a great deal in comparison. Bundling combines complementary services into a single package at a slightly discounted rate to increase perceived value.

**Horario pricing page strategy:**
1. **Lead with the highest tier** (e.g., Multi-Location Plan at S/ 149/month)
2. **Position the core paid tier** as the "smart choice" in the middle
3. **Make Free visible but limited** — showing what they miss reinforces the upgrade

### 2.4 Impact of Currency Volatility on Pricing Decisions

Currency volatility is a unique LatAm SaaS challenge:

- **Peru (PEN):** Relatively stable but politically sensitive
- **Colombia (COP):** Significant volatility vs. USD
- **Mexico (MXN):** Nearshoring boom has strengthened the peso, but SMBs remain cautious
- **Brazil (BRL):** High volatility historically

For multi-currency SaaS pricing, the recommendation is to show prices in the user's local currency based on exchange rates, but the actual charge can remain in USD — this helps with "mental math" but doesn't solve the problem of foreign transaction fees or exchange rate volatility.

**Horario recommendation:** Price in **local currency (PEN for Peru)** with fixed S/ pricing — not USD-converted amounts that fluctuate. Peruvian SMB owners budget in soles. A price of "S/ 89/mes" feels real and manageable; "$24.50/month" feels foreign and unstable.

### 2.5 Annual Discount Sensitivity

Most discounts on annual SaaS plans fall between 15–20%, and all companies that offer yearly plans offer discounts on them.

SaaS month-to-month contracts have higher churn (18% annually) than the 8% average churn for 2+ year contracts.

For LatAm SMBs, **annual plans face structural resistance** because:
1. Cash flow is unpredictable (especially for restaurants, retail, hospitality)
2. Trust in the vendor is not yet established at purchase
3. Informal economy players may not have cards that support recurring billing

**Recommendation:** Offer annual plans at **25–30% discount** (above the global norm) to overcome the higher trust hurdle. Only promote annual plans **after a user has completed 2+ scheduling cycles** (proven value delivery).

## Part 3: Informal Economy & Trust Issues

### 3.1 Informality Rates in Peru

According to INEI (Peru's national statistics institute), 70.9% of workers in Peru were informal at the close of 2024. This rate, while having declined slightly, remains among the highest in the region, well above comparable LatAm economies according to ILO data.

Peru's Ministry of Production reports that the SME segment represents 99.5% of all formal companies in Peru — of which 95.6% are microenterprises, 3.8% small, and 0.1% medium — and more than 2.1 million formal MiPymes operate in the Peruvian market.

**The informality paradox for Horario:** Many of Horario's target users — restaurant owners, retail managers — operate businesses that are partially or fully informal. This means:
- They may lack corporate credit cards for SaaS subscriptions
- They may be skeptical of cloud tools that "store their data"
- They pay with cash, Yape, or bank transfer — not subscription billing
- They may not have a formal RUC (tax ID) or want to be formally invoiced

**Strategy:** Enable **Yape/Plin QR-code payment for monthly subscriptions** (manual or automated via API), not just card billing. Offer the option to pay via bank transfer with a monthly boleta (informal receipt) rather than only facturas.

### 3.3 Data Privacy Laws: LPDP (Peru) & LGPD (Brazil)

**Peru — LPDP (Ley de Protección de Datos Personales):**

On November 30, 2024, Supreme Decree No. 016-2024-JUS was published, approving a new Regulation to Peru's Personal Data Protection Law (LPDP), which entered into force on March 30, 2025 — revoking the previous 2013 version. It establishes specific rules for territorial application of the LPDP regarding non-domiciled companies.

The 2025 LPDP regulation introduces phased requirements for appointing Data Protection Officers based on company revenue. The new regulation introduces proactive accountability obligations — requiring organizations to demonstrate compliance through technical, legal, and organizational measures.

Under the new regulation, compliance timelines are phased: large companies must comply within 1 year, medium companies within 2 years, and small companies have extended timelines.

**Brazil — LGPD (Lei Geral de Proteção de Dados):**
Brazil's LGPD (2020) mirrors GDPR requirements. As Horario expands to Brazil, it must appoint a Data Protection Officer (Encarregado), maintain records of processing activities, and enable data subject rights (access, deletion, portability).

**Horario compliance action items:**
- Display **LPDP compliance badge** prominently on landing page and app
- Implement **data subject request flow** (user can request their data deletion)
- Ensure **Supabase RLS (Row Level Security)** isolates tenant data (already in stack)
- Add a **privacy policy in plain-language Spanish** explaining what employee data is stored

### 4.2 Digital Wallet Adoption — The New Payment Rail

**Peru — Yape & Plin:**

Peru's digital wallet adoption has surged in recent years, with Yape and Plin each serving approximately 14 million users in 2024.

In the first semester of 2023, the Central Reserve Bank of Peru registered 397 million digital transactions, including the CCE system and digital wallets like Yape and Plin — representing an increase of 76% compared to 2022.

In Peru, Yape and Plin have become everyday verbs ("yapear/plinear"), and their interoperability is pushing wallets to the front of the checkout flow — often ahead of cards for domestic spending.

**Colombia — Nequi & Daviplata:**

Nequi recorded over 13 million users in H1 2025, with more than 503,000 loans disbursed in 2024, setting a record of processing 66 million transactions in a single day on June 28, 2024. DaviPlata, operated by Davivienda, has 19 million customers.

The Financial Superintendence of Colombia reported that real-time transfers through digital wallets, during the second half of 2024, grew 231%.

**The broader LatAm trend:**

Crucially, this shift toward wallets and transfers is visible not only in Brazil and Argentina, but also in Peru and Chile, where wallets and transfers increasingly mirror offline payment habits online. Methods like Yape and PagoEfectivo in Peru, Khipu in Chile, Transferencias 3.0 in Argentina, and Nequi and Bre-B in Colombia are not niche options — they are mainstream rails.

On November 30, 2024, Peru published Supreme Decree No. 016-2024-JUS — the new Regulation of the Personal Data Protection Law (LPDP) — which entered into force on March 30, 2025, revoking the previous 2013 version.
